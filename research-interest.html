Research & publications

Research interest

My research lies at the interface of mathematics and biology, with a focus on how infectious diseases spread through populations and how they can be controlled. I build compartmental models of diseases such as monkeypox, dengue, Nipah virus, HFMD, COVID-19 and avian influenza. I formulate them with ordinary, fractional-order, delay and stochastic differential equations, partial differential equations, and analyse them with the tools of dynamical systems: stability and bifurcation theory, threshold dynamics and numerical simulation.

A central question in my work is how features of real infections change the course of an outbreak. These include memory effects, captured through Caputo fractional derivatives, and incubation and gestation delays. They also include the movement of individuals across space, which can produce Turing patterns, and seasonal variation in transmission. To study seasonality, I am developing non-autonomous models with time-periodic coefficients, in which the basic reproduction number is defined through the next-generation operator of the periodic system. I also work on predator–prey and eco-epidemic systems, where fear, harvesting and disease interact.

Alongside the analysis, I use optimal control, sensitivity analysis and cost-effectiveness analysis to design and compare interventions such as vaccination, treatment and awareness campaigns, with the aim of supporting public-health decisions.

Main themes

  • Infectious disease modellingMonkeypox, dengue, Nipah, hand, foot and mouth disease (HFMD), COVID-19 and avian influenza.

    I build compartmental models (SIR, SEIR, SEIQR and host–vector) that follow how people move between being susceptible, exposed, infected, quarantined and recovered. For each model I derive the basic reproduction number, study the stability of the disease-free and endemic equilibria, and use simulations to compare control strategies.

    Across these models I include features that shape real outbreaks: media awareness, waning immunity, quarantine, and saturated or nonlinear incidence and treatment.

  • Fractional-order differential equationsCaputo-type models that carry memory effects into epidemic dynamics.

    In a classical epidemic model, the rate of change of each compartment depends only on the present state. Many biological processes, however, carry a memory: immunity wanes, behaviour adapts and infection responds to past exposure. I replace the ordinary derivative with the Caputo fractional derivative of order 0 < α ≤ 1,

    CDαx(t) = 1⁄Γ(1−α) ∫0t (t−s)−α x′(s) ds,   0 < α < 1,

    so that the present rate is a weighted average over the whole past, with weights that decay like a power law. At α = 1 the model reduces to the classical one. The Caputo form keeps initial conditions in their usual form, which makes the models easy to fit to data.

  • Optimal controlTreatment and vaccination strategies from Pontryagin’s maximum principle, ranked by cost-effectiveness.

    Once a model shows how a disease spreads, the next question is how best to stop it with limited resources. I treat interventions such as vaccination, treatment, quarantine and awareness as time-dependent controls ui(t), bounded by 0 ≤ ui(t) ≤ uimax, and look for the schedule that minimises a cost functional such as

    J(u) = ∫0T [ A1E(t) + A2I(t) + ½ ∑i Biui2(t) ] dt,

    which balances the burden of infection against the cost of the interventions.

    The aim is practical guidance: which intervention to prioritise, when to start it, and how its intensity should change over the course of an outbreak.

  • Spatio-temporal dynamicsReaction–diffusion and cross-diffusion models, Turing instability and pattern formation.

    Diseases and populations do not mix uniformly: individuals move, habitats differ, and infection spreads from place to place. I model this with reaction–diffusion systems of the form

    ∂u/∂t = DΔu + f(u),   x ∈ Ω,   with zero-flux boundary conditions,

    where f describes the local epidemic or ecological interactions and the diffusion matrix D describes movement. When D has off-diagonal terms, the system includes cross-diffusion: the movement of one group responds to the density of another, for example preys moving away from predators.

    • Turing instability: conditions under which an equilibrium that is stable without movement becomes unstable once diffusion is added. In practice, this means some spatial wavenumber k makes det(J − k2D) < 0, where J is the Jacobian of f at that equilibrium. This diffusion-driven instability gives rise to stationary spatial patterns such as spots, stripes and labyrinths.
    • Beyond stationary patterns: Hopf and Turing–Hopf interactions, non-stationary and chaotic spatio-temporal patterns, and methods to control the chaos.
  • Stability, chaos and bifurcationHopf bifurcation, delay-induced oscillations and chaos control in eco-epidemiological systems.

    A model is useful only if we understand its long-term behaviour: whether an infection dies out, settles to a steady level, keeps returning in waves, or behaves erratically. I study how this behaviour changes as parameters vary, and what each change means biologically.

    • Stability: local stability of the disease-free and endemic equilibria from the linearised system, using the Routh–Hurwitz criteria, and global stability through Lyapunov functions and LaSalle’s invariance principle. The basic reproduction number R0 is the usual threshold: below 1 the infection dies out, above 1 it persists.
    • Hopf bifurcation: the point at which a pair of complex eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis and a steady state gives way to sustained oscillations, which in a population appear as recurring outbreaks. Normal-form analysis determines the direction of the bifurcation and the stability of the resulting periodic orbits.
    • Delay-induced oscillations: incubation and gestation periods enter as time delays τ. From the transcendental characteristic equation I find the critical delay at which purely imaginary roots appear, check the transversality condition, and show how a longer delay can destabilise an otherwise stable equilibrium. Sci. Rep. 2025, MATCOM 2027
    • Fractional order and delay together: Hopf bifurcation in fractional-order systems, where both the memory order and the delay act as bifurcation parameters. IJDC 2026, EPJ ST
    • Chaos and its control: identifying chaotic behaviour through bifurcation diagrams, phase portraits and Lyapunov exponents, and designing controls that steer the system back to regular dynamics. MATCOM 2026, IJBC 2023

    In eco-epidemiological systems, where disease spreads within interacting predator and prey populations, these tools show how infection can stabilise or destabilise a community, and when small changes in a parameter lead to large changes in outcome.

  • Sensitivity analysisPRCC-based global sensitivity analysis and stochastic stability.

    Epidemic parameters such as contact rates, recovery rates and vaccine efficacy are rarely known exactly, and real populations are subject to random fluctuations. I ask which parameters matter most for an outbreak, and whether the model’s predictions still hold when the environment is noisy.

    • Local sensitivity: the normalised forward sensitivity index of the basic reproduction number, Υp = (∂R0/∂p)(p/R0), which gives the percentage change in R0 for a 1% change in a parameter p.
    • Global sensitivity (PRCC): sampling all parameters together by Latin hypercube sampling, then computing partial rank correlation coefficients between each parameter and outputs such as R0 or the number infected, after removing the effects of the other parameters. Unlike local indices, this captures nonlinear, monotone effects across the whole plausible parameter range.
    • Stochastic stability: adding environmental noise to the model as white-noise perturbations, which turns it into a system of stochastic differential equations. Using stochastic Lyapunov functions, I find conditions under which the equilibria remain stable under noise, and confirm them with numerical simulations of sample paths.

    Together, these methods show which parameters are the most effective targets for control, and how reliable the model’s conclusions are when the data are uncertain.
